# Progressive dinner at Christmas or New Year's Eve: a festive how-to

December is peak progressive dinner season. Neighbours want to see each other, everyone cooks elaborately anyway, and an evening through three warm houses is exactly what a cold month needs. But a holiday edition needs a few tweaks to the usual recipe. Here is what changes — and what doesn't.

## Pick the right day (and avoid the wrong one)

- **Christmas Day and Boxing Day are tricky**: family obligations win. The golden dates are the **weekend before Christmas** or the days between Christmas and New Year — full festive mood, empty calendars.
- **New Year's Eve works brilliantly**: many people want an alternative to the same old party. Just plan the courses so everyone reaches the closing venue well before midnight.
- Send the invitation in **mid-November**: December calendars fill up early.

## The adapted New Year's Eve schedule

| Time | Item |
|---|---|
| 6:00 pm | Aperitif at the closing venue |
| 7:00 pm | Starter at host 1 |
| 8:15 pm | Main course at host 2 |
| 9:45 pm | Dessert at host 3 |
| 11:00 pm | Everyone to the closing venue — count down together |

The secret: **the closing party is sacred.** Nobody wants to be walking outside at 11:55 pm, so keep the final leg short and agree that dessert genuinely ends at 11:00 pm.

## Festive menu ideas that survive the schedule

The usual rules apply twice over: preparable, wait-proof, served in one go. Festive versions:

- **Starter**: smoked salmon with blinis (cold, done), chestnut soup with truffle oil, or a carpaccio board.
- **Main**: venison stew or boeuf bourguignon (improves the longer it sits). Venison medallions are risky — they demand minute-by-minute stove work. Vegetarian: mushroom wellington, kept warm in the oven.
- **Dessert**: pavlova (bases ready, assembled in five minutes), tiramisu in glasses, or a cheese board with Christmas bread.
- **New Year's Eve**: doughnuts or oliebollen at the closing party — naturally.

## Practical: dark, cold and busy calendars

- **Lighting**: participants walk in the dark. Ask hosts to switch on the outside light and include house numbers in the route info.
- **Warm transitions**: a 15-minute walk at -2 °C feels different from June. Keep legs under 8 minutes or suggest bikes.
- **Mulled wine stop**: a halfway stop with mulled wine or hot chocolate on the longest leg turns the walk itself into part of the party.
- **Alcohol and midnight**: for the New Year's edition, the bubbly belongs at the closing venue — not at every course. That way everyone experiences midnight clearly.

## The planning: tighter than usual

Holiday editions tend to attract more participants than normal (neighbours invite neighbours) — and that is exactly when planning by hand becomes an evening-filling puzzle: who cooks which course, who sits with whom without repeat encounters, and do the walking distances still work? A planner works that out in seconds, checks the distances and prints the envelope cards — or shares the route digitally, revealing each next address only when the previous course ends. Exactly what you want when it is freezing outside and the wine is open inside.

## Conclusion

A progressive dinner may be the loveliest way to celebrate Christmas or New Year's Eve with your neighbourhood: intimate at the table and still a party with everyone. Set the date early, keep the routes short and warm, protect the countdown — and don't let the seating plan become your own Christmas puzzle.
